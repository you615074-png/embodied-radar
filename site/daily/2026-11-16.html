Hidden Start-Delay Randomization Makes Self-Play Handover Robust

PaperRLWhen Will You Hand It Over? A Hidden Start Delay Makes Self-Play Handover Robust to Unfamiliar Partners

What it is: A paper (CoRL 2026 HumanRLI Workshop) by Mohammad Khoshnazar, Mike Degany, Deyuan Qu, Qing Yang and Matyas Szanto. They train a Unitree G1 humanoid and a learned "giver" to hand over a parcel using alternating PPO self-play in physics simulation. The problem: the giver always offers the parcel immediately, so the receiver only learns one fixed rendezvous. When a partner it has never met starts a second later, success drops from 95% to 6%. Their fix, hidden start-delay randomization (HSD), adds a random delay to the learned partner's start — a one-line rule the receiver never observes. After 300 more updates, success with unseen scripted partners rises from 31% to 80%, with high-force contacts cut three- to five-fold.

Why it matters: Self-play is a cheap way to learn interaction without human demonstrations, but policies can quietly overfit to their training partner. HSD is a small, interpretable change that targets that exact failure, and the paper's paired interventions make the cause-and-effect clear.

Can you try it without a robot? Yes, in simulation only. The authors released code at github.com/mkhoshnam/Selfplay-PPO-handover. Training PPO self-play for a G1 will be slow on a 16GB Apple Silicon Mac, so a cloud GPU is the realistic option; reading the reward and randomization code is a good first step. Note: the paper reports simulation results only, with no real-robot experiments.

Try it without a robot: Train the PPO self-play G1 handover in the repo's Isaac/MuJoCo sim; Mac is slow, use cloud GPU or just read the reward code first.
